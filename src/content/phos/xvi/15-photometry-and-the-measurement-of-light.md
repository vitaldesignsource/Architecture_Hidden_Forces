Radiometry measures electromagnetic radiation in physical units. Radiant flux is measured in watts; irradiance measures radiant power arriving per unit area; radiance incorporates direction and projected area.

Photometry weights optical radiation according to standardised models of human visual sensitivity. Luminous flux is measured in lumens, luminous intensity in candelas, illuminance in lux, and luminance in candelas per square metre. [NIST maintains](https://www.nist.gov/pml/sensor-science/photometry) the standards and calibration services for these quantities.

This distinction matters because physical energy and perceived brightness are not identical. The eye is more sensitive to some wavelengths than others, and its sensitivity changes between bright and dim conditions. Equal radiant power at two wavelengths may therefore produce unequal visual brightness.

## What an instrument can and cannot answer

Every measurement also requires an operational definition. An instrument measures a specified property within a defined range. It does not measure "light in every possible sense". A lux meter cannot detect meaning, spiritual presence, beauty, or etheric organisation because those are not the quantities it was designed to register.

This does not establish those other qualities as real, but it prevents an invalid argument: failure of one instrument to detect something outside its domain is not proof of nonexistence. The proper question is whether the proposed phenomenon produces consistent effects that can be operationally distinguished from alternatives.

Science advances by converting qualities into stable relations that can be compared. Metaphysical research would similarly benefit from clearly described conditions, repeated observations, independent witnesses, [[xi-27|phenomenological records]], and attention to failures - not because every reality must become a number, but because disciplined description protects meaning from fantasy.
