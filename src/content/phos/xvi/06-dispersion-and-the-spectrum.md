Dispersion occurs because a material's refractive index varies with wavelength. Different wavelengths therefore bend by different amounts when passing through a prism, water droplet, or other dispersive medium. What appeared as unified white light can emerge as a visible spectrum.

[[xv-89|Newton]] demonstrated that a prism does not simply stain white light. The separated colours can be recombined to form white light again. This established that ordinary white light contains a distribution of spectral components rather than being an absolutely simple optical substance.

A [[vii-26|rainbow]] results through a combination of refraction, internal reflection, and dispersion in water droplets. Its colours are continuous even though human language divides them into named regions. The familiar sevenfold division is historically and symbolically powerful, but the physical spectrum does not contain seven sharply separated bands.

## Spectral signatures

Spectroscopy generalises this principle. Atoms and molecules absorb or emit particular wavelengths according to their allowed energy transitions. Their spectral lines function like signatures. By examining distant light, scientists can infer chemical composition, temperature, density, velocity, magnetic fields, and other properties of sources that cannot be physically reached.

Dispersion supplies an exact image of differentiated unity. A source can be one at one level and multiple at another. The prism does not manufacture all distinctions, but it provides the condition through which latent differences become visible; [OpenStax treats the phenomenon](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-5-dispersion) as the spreading of white light into its full range of wavelengths.

Within the Architecture of Hidden Forces, the spectrum offers a disciplined model for [[i-13|the One Light and its many participations]]. The rays are not fragments severed from all unity. They are distinguishable modes within a larger luminous whole. Recombination does not erase differentiation; it restores coherence among differentiated components.
