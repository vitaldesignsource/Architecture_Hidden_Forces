## Concluding doctrine: what the measurements settle, and what they leave open

Twenty-six entries of optics, electromagnetism, quantum theory, photometry, colour vision, astronomy, relativity, photosynthesis, bioluminescence, circadian biology, and neuroscience establish something the rest of the encyclopaedia depends on: that light has a physical account which is detailed, quantitative, predictive, and true.

The system does not need that account to be incomplete. Its own claims are not lodged in the gaps. Where a physical mechanism exists, it is real at its own level, and an entry that reaches past it for a subtler cause without engaging it has not deepened the subject.

What the physics does not do is settle questions it was not built to ask. Photometry measures how much light arrives; it says nothing about whether the arrival was truthful. Spectroscopy resolves a source into wavelengths; it does not resolve a symbol into its meanings. This is not a deficiency in physics. It is the boundary of its question, which [[xvi-25|the entry on what modern physics does and does not say]] traces line by line.

Two failures follow from ignoring that boundary. Borrowing a physical term to lend weight to a metaphysical claim — quantum, field, frequency, vibration — is the commonest, and it makes the claim harder to test rather than easier. The opposite failure is treating a measured mechanism as the whole of the matter and calling the remaining question meaningless.

> A measurement is not a refutation of what it did not measure, and a metaphysics is not confirmed by a vocabulary it has borrowed.
