Nearly everything known about distant astronomical objects comes through radiation. Telescopes collect photons that have crossed immense distances, carrying information about their sources and the media through which they travelled.

Spectral lines reveal elemental and molecular composition. Doppler shifts reveal motion toward or away from the observer. Polarisation reveals magnetic fields, scattering environments, and geometric orientation. Brightness variations reveal rotating bodies, pulsating stars, eclipsing systems, planetary transits, and explosive events. Infrared observations penetrate dust; radio waves map cold gas; X-rays reveal hot plasma and extreme gravitational environments. [NASA emphasises](https://imagine.gsfc.nasa.gov/science/toolbox/multiwavelength1.html) that different wavelength regions disclose different properties of cosmic objects.

## An instrument of memory

Because light travels at a finite speed, astronomy observes the past. The sun is seen as it was minutes ago; nearby stars as they were years ago; distant galaxies as they were millions or billions of years ago. A telescope is therefore an instrument of luminous memory.

Astronomical imaging is also interpretive. Many celebrated images assign visible colours to wavelengths humans cannot see. This does not make them fraudulent. The images are translations of measured data into a perceptual form. Responsible presentation explains the translation.

Within the encyclopaedia, astronomy offers the strongest physical example of residual light. The source may have changed or ceased to exist while its radiation continues through space. Yet astronomical afterlight should not be equated automatically with the psychic or spiritual survival of a being. It establishes the broader principle that effects and information can remain in transit after the originating configuration has passed away - a physical parallel to [[ii-28|the Ossuary of Living Forms]].
