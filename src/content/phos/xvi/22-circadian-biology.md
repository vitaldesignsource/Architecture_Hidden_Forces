Circadian rhythms are internally generated cycles of approximately twenty-four hours. They persist in constant conditions, but environmental signals - especially light - reset them so that biological time remains aligned with day and night.

In humans, specialised intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells contain melanopsin and respond strongly to particular portions of the spectrum. They send information through the retinohypothalamic tract to the suprachiasmatic nucleus, or SCN, in the hypothalamus. The SCN coordinates daily rhythms across the nervous, endocrine, metabolic, and behavioural systems; [the review literature](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2885915/) traces the architecture and projections of these cells.

Light exposure in the morning can advance the circadian phase, while evening or nighttime exposure can delay it, depending upon timing, intensity, duration, spectrum, and individual history. Melatonin secretion, sleep propensity, alertness, body temperature, hormone release, digestion, and gene expression participate in this temporal architecture.

## Light that is not seen

The effect of light is therefore not reducible to seeing. A person may receive biologically powerful illumination without consciously attending to it. Light becomes time-information within the organism.

Circadian biology provides a physical basis for taking [[xiv-6|dawn, noon, sunset, and midnight]] seriously as qualitatively different biological conditions. Their ritual meanings are not created from nothing. They are built upon real changes in solar angle, spectrum, temperature, hormone regulation, alertness, metabolism, and environmental activity.

This does not prove that every traditional planetary hour has a distinct measurable mechanism. It does show that the organism is temporally porous: celestial cycles enter physiology and organise life from within.
