Colour temperature describes the colour of an ideal blackbody radiator at a specified absolute temperature. As the body becomes hotter, its visible glow moves from red through orange and yellow toward white and blue-white.

The definition, the temperature of the blackbody whose colour a source matches, is given in [Britannica's article on colour temperature](https://www.britannica.com/science/colour-temperature).

This produces a familiar linguistic reversal. Light sources around 2700 kelvin appear visually "warm" because they are yellow or orange. Sources around 5000 to 6500 kelvin appear "cool" because they are white or bluish. Physically, however, the higher colour temperature corresponds to the hotter blackbody.

Many artificial sources are not true blackbody radiators. Their correlated colour temperature, or CCT, identifies the blackbody colour most closely resembling their chromatic appearance. Two sources can have the same CCT while possessing very different spectral power distributions and rendering colours differently.

## One quantity among many

Colour temperature is therefore not a complete account of a light's quality. It says little by itself about intensity, spectrum, flicker, colour rendering, polarisation, coherence, or biological effect.

This scientific distinction is useful for the Encyclopaedia of Luminous Qualities. "Warm light" can refer to a subjective colour quality, measured blackbody temperature, thermal output, emotional association, or etheric warmth. These meanings overlap in experience but should not be treated as identical.
