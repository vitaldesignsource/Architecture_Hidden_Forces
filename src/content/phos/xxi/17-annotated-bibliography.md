The bibliography explains why each work matters rather than merely listing titles. Entries may be classified as primary edition, translation, philological study, historical synthesis, philosophical interpretation, scientific work, esoteric source, or comparative study.

## Stating the limitation

Annotations also indicate limitations. An older translation may be historically influential but philologically outdated. A pioneering occult study may preserve valuable correspondences while blending traditions too freely. A modern scientific paper may be rigorous within measurement yet irrelevant to the metaphysical conclusions sometimes drawn from it.

None of these is a reason to exclude a work. A translation that shaped a century of reading has to be listed even where a better one exists, because the tradition it produced is itself part of the evidence. What the annotation must not do is let the reader mistake influence for accuracy.

## Including what resists

The strongest bibliography includes works that support, challenge, and complicate the encyclopaedia. The Architecture of Hidden Forces becomes more robust when readers can identify where it inherits, synthesises, departs, and makes original claims. A bibliography assembled only from agreement would leave every one of those boundaries invisible, and an architecture whose boundaries cannot be seen cannot be tested.
