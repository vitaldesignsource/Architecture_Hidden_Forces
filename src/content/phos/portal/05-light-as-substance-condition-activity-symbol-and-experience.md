Different civilisations have assigned light different kinds of reality. Some have treated it as an emanated substance. Others have understood it as the actuality of a transparent medium, the activity of fire, a stream of particles, a geometrical ray, a divine energy, a living power, or the visible expression of an invisible intelligence.

The portal therefore distinguishes five principal ways of speaking about light.

**Light as substance** treats light as something that proceeds, travels, becomes mixed, can be captured, divided, purified, or returned. [[xv-38|Manichaean]] descriptions of light imprisoned in matter strongly employ this model, while modern physics gives the term an entirely different technical meaning through photons and electromagnetic fields.

**Light as condition** understands illumination as a state through which visibility becomes possible. [[xv-21|Aristotle's description of light as the actuality of the transparent]] is an important example. Light is not simply an additional object inserted into the world; it is the actualisation of a capacity within a medium.

**Light as activity** emphasises what illumination does. It reveals, awakens, distinguishes, warms, nourishes, exposes, guides, purifies, transforms, or organises. Here light is understood through operation rather than substance.

**Light as symbol** uses visible illumination to communicate truths about knowledge, divinity, consciousness, justice, life, immortality, purity, authority, or revelation. Symbolic light is not therefore “merely decorative.” A powerful symbol gathers multiple orders of meaning into a perceptible form. Yet its symbolic significance should not automatically be converted into a physical claim.

**Light as experience** concerns luminosity as directly encountered. This includes ordinary seeing, aesthetic experience, meditative radiance, visionary phenomena, dreams, afterimages, entoptic light, neurological aura, and reports of spiritual illumination. The fact that an experience occurs is distinct from the interpretation placed upon it. The portal preserves this distinction so the experience can be examined without prematurely reducing or exalting it.

A single tradition may use several of these models simultaneously. The task is not to force one definition upon all uses of light, but to discover which mode is operative in each passage, ritual, image, doctrine, or experience.
