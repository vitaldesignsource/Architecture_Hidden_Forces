Every entry in the encyclopaedia carries one or more of seven labels, shown above its first line. They answer one question before anything else is read: what kind of claim is this?

The question matters because the encyclopaedia holds four different things side by side, and they are easy to confuse. A passage of Plotinus, a measurement of ultraweak photon emission from living tissue, a meditator's report of a stable white light, and a law the system proposes about the capacity of a receiver are not the same kind of statement, do not answer to the same tests, and should not be believed on the same grounds. The Architecture asks of any claim whether it is empirical, traditional, symbolic, metaphysical, or speculative. The labels are that question made visible on every page.

## The seven

**Primary Text.** A passage from a source, quoted or closely paraphrased, with its provenance given. The entry may interpret it, but the passage is the thing, and the reader can go to it.

**Historical Doctrine.** What a tradition, school, or author taught, stated as theirs. That Suhrawardī held every degree of being to be a greater or lesser intensity of one light is a historical fact about Suhrawardī, whatever one thinks of the doctrine; an entry so labelled reports it without asserting it.

**Scientific Finding.** A result of measurement or experiment, with the limits of what it establishes stated as carefully as the result. The finding that cells emit photons is a finding; what it means for a doctrine of vital light is not, and an entry that runs the two together is mislabelled.

**Phenomenological Report.** A description of experience as it was experienced, before any claim about its cause. A stable golden light seen with the eyes closed is a report. That it was a being, or a nerve, or a grace, is a further step the report does not take.

**Architecture of Hidden Forces Doctrine.** A position the system itself holds and is prepared to defend. These are the entries the rest exist to test, and the label is a promise that the claim is being made, not merely reported.

**Comparative Interpretation.** A reading that sets two or more sources beside each other and says what the comparison shows. It is the encyclopaedia's most frequent kind of entry and its most fallible, since a correspondence can be found between almost anything by someone determined to find it; the label asks the reader to check the comparison, not to accept it.

**Speculative Synthesis.** A proposal that goes past what any source or finding supports, marked so that it can be tested rather than believed. The system does not forbid speculation. It forbids speculation that does not say it is speculating.

## How they combine

Most entries carry two labels, because most do two things: report a doctrine and interpret it, or describe an experience and set it beside others. An entry with a single label is usually the simpler kind, a text or a finding or a report on its own. An entry that would need four is probably two entries.

An entry never carries the Doctrine label in order to borrow authority for the material beside it. Where a historical teaching and a position of the system agree, the entry says so and carries both; where they part, it says that too.

:: The labels are a discipline for the writer before they are a guide for the reader. An entry that cannot be labelled has not yet decided what it is claiming.
