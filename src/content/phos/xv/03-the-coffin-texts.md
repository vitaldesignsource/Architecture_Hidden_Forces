The Coffin Texts emerged principally during the First Intermediate Period and Middle Kingdom, approximately 2100–1650 BCE. They were written on coffins, sarcophagi, tomb walls, masks, and other funerary objects. Older Pyramid Text material continued to circulate alongside newer compositions, producing a greatly expanded body of mortuary knowledge. The University of Chicago's editions emphasise that Middle Kingdom coffins frequently preserved both contemporary Coffin Texts and copies of older Pyramid Texts, showing continuity rather than simple replacement — see James P. Allen's *The Egyptian Coffin Texts, Volume 8: Middle Kingdom Copies of Pyramid Texts* in the [Oriental Institute Publications series](https://isac.uchicago.edu/research/publications/oriental-institute-publications-oip).

Access to elaborate funerary texts broadened beyond the king, although it remained closely connected with social status and the resources required for a prepared burial. This expansion is sometimes called the "democratisation of the afterlife", but the term can be misleading if it implies universal or equal access. What clearly changed was the range of people represented as possessing ritual knowledge and claiming participation in transformations formerly expressed predominantly in royal settings.

## Light must be navigated

The Coffin Texts intensified the cartography of the beyond. The deceased required names, spells, transformations, routes, gates, guardians, provisions, and protection. The Book of Two Ways, preserved within the Coffin Text corpus, presents paths through a dangerous otherworldly region, combining spatial knowledge with ritual power. Light must be navigated, not merely admired.

The coffin itself became a luminous architecture. It enclosed the body in protected darkness while covering that darkness with names, eyes, images, routes, and utterances. The deceased rested inside a material womb of transformative knowledge. The outer senses were closed, but another form of perception was being constructed.

Within the system, the Coffin Texts reveal that spiritual survival requires mediation. The deceased must possess a vessel, an identity, an orientation, a language of recognition, and a field through which transformation can proceed. Light does not simply arrive and erase death; it reorganises the dead into a being capable of acting beyond death.
