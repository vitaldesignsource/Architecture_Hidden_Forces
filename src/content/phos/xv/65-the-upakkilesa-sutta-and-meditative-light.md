The Upakkilesa Sutta, [Majjhima Nikāya 128](https://suttacentral.net/mn128/en/sujato), presents a remarkably practical discussion of meditative luminosity. Several monks report that during diligent meditation they perceive both light and forms, but the light and visions repeatedly disappear.

The Buddha explains that he encountered the same difficulty before his awakening. Rather than treating the appearance of light as proof that the practice was complete, he investigated the conditions causing concentration to collapse.

The disturbances include doubt, inattention, sloth and drowsiness, fear, excitement, boredom or inertia, excessive energy, deficient energy, longing, perception of diversity, and excessive attention to forms. When one of these imperfections arose, the light and vision disappeared.

## Diagnosis rather than dismissal

This account takes meditative light seriously. The practitioners are not told that nothing happened. Their perception of light and forms is accepted as an actual component of contemplative experience. The problem is instability and incomplete mastery.

The Buddha's method is diagnostic. He observes the arising factor, identifies it, modifies the mind, and learns how to prevent the disturbance from overthrowing concentration. The light becomes an instrument through which subtle imbalances are revealed.

Excess is as important as deficiency. Too little energy produces dullness, but too much produces agitation. Excessive fascination with forms can be as disruptive as inability to perceive them. The correct condition is not maximal intensity but balanced continuity.

The discourse also distinguishes the extent of light from the extent of visionary form. The practitioner may perceive limited light and limited forms, immeasurable light and limited forms, limited light and immeasurable forms, or both immeasurably. Attention, concentration, and the scope of the meditative field interact.

## Separable qualities

This provides an ancient phenomenology of luminous experience. Intensity, spatial extension, stability, content, clarity, and duration are separable qualities. A vision can become brighter without becoming more truthful, or broader without becoming more stable.

Within the present system, the Upakkilesa Sutta offers a model for [[xi-27|recording and testing luminous phenomena]]. The practitioner should note the light's onset, its tattvic coloration, the condition of breath and body, the emotional atmosphere, the form perceived, the degree of control, and the aftereffects.

Its most important lesson is that spiritual light requires equilibrium of the receiver. An authentic current may be present while the vessel remains unable to sustain it. The disappearance of light does not necessarily prove that the original perception was false; it may expose a weakness in attention, energy, emotional regulation, or subtle-body coherence.
