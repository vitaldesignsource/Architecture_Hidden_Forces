Claudius Ptolemy's *Optics*, composed in the second century CE, united geometrical analysis with experiment and a more developed psychology of perception. The original Greek is lost. Much of the work survives through a Latin translation made from an Arabic translation, and the first book is missing. Its surviving condition requires caution, but its importance is unmistakable.

Ptolemy retained a visual-ray model, treating sight as an active relation directed from the eye. Yet he did not restrict optics to abstract geometry. He studied binocular vision, apparent size, distance, colour, illumination, mirrors, reflection, refraction, and perceptual error.

## Reflection

In [[iii-20|reflection]], the visual ray encounters a surface it cannot penetrate and rebounds. Ptolemy analysed plane, convex, concave, cylindrical, and other mirrors. He investigated why reflected images appear behind the mirror and how curvature alters size, orientation, and position.

His analysis recognises that the apparent location of an image results from the perceiver mentally extending the reflected ray backward in a straight line. Perception interprets a broken path as though it were direct. The [[iii-14|mirror]] therefore reveals that seeing includes unconscious geometrical judgment.

## Refraction

[[iii-21|Refraction]] occurs when the ray passes from one transparent medium into another and changes direction. Ptolemy experimented with air, water, and glass, measuring angles of incidence and refraction. His work preserves the earliest surviving numerical tables of refraction. The values do not express the later sine law associated with Snell and Ibn Sahl, and historians debate how directly every value was experimentally obtained. Nevertheless, his systematic measurements represent a major advance.

Ptolemy understood reflection and refraction as related forms of a ray's deviation. Reflection occurs when a resistant surface turns the ray back; refraction occurs when the ray penetrates a transparent medium but is deflected by the difference between media. This systemic relationship is emphasised in modern histories of Greek optics, among them [the "Greek Optics" chapter of the Cambridge History of Science](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-history-of-science/greek-optics/946ADBA7185414D23D20615633835440).

He also distinguished sensation from perceptual interpretation. The visual faculty judges distance, size, shape, motion, and position from the pattern of rays, but these judgments can fail. Optical illusion is not simply false sensation. It arises when the perceptual system interprets genuine visual relations according to ordinary assumptions that do not fit the unusual situation.

Within the system, Ptolemy provides an advanced anatomy of mediation. When a ray crosses a threshold between media, its direction changes. The receiver may then reconstruct the source incorrectly. This becomes a physical analogue for visionary and psychic [[xi-25|discernment]]: a real influence can be received through a distorted path, and a genuine appearance can be assigned to the wrong location or cause.
