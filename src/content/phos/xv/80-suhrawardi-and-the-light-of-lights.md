Shihāb al-Dīn Yaḥyā al-Suhrawardī, the twelfth-century founder of the Illuminationist or Ishrāqī school, transformed light from an analogy of knowledge into the governing principle of an entire metaphysics. His principal work, *Ḥikmat al-Ishrāq*, the Philosophy of Illumination, presents reality as a hierarchy of luminous and obscured presences.

For Suhrawardī, nothing is more evident than light. Light requires no definition because every definition depends upon something more evident than the thing defined, and nothing is more manifest than manifestation itself. Light reveals itself through its very presence. What is conscious and self-aware is therefore luminous in a primary sense: it is present to itself without requiring an intermediary representation.

## Nūr al-Anwār

At the summit stands *Nūr al-Anwār*, the Light of Lights. It is absolutely independent, unlimited, and self-subsisting. It receives no illumination from another source, while every subordinate light depends directly or indirectly upon it. The Light of Lights is not the brightest member within a class of luminous beings. It is the source that makes the entire hierarchy possible.

From it proceed innumerable immaterial lights. Their differentiation does not divide the source into fragments or diminish it. One light can illuminate another without losing its own radiance. Procession is therefore non-depletive. [[i-12|The source remains what it is]] while derivative lights receive according to their rank and capacity.

Suhrawardī distinguishes self-subsisting immaterial lights from accidental lights that appear in bodies. Bodies are not independent positive forces opposed equally to light. They are "barriers" or "dusky substances" whose opacity limits manifestation. Matter is characterised by its inability to disclose itself fully. Yet it can receive accidental light and become a site of appearance.

## Knowledge by presence

Knowledge is similarly illumination. Suhrawardī's "knowledge by presence" means that certain realities are known because they are immediately present to the knower, not because the mind has constructed a detached representation of them. Self-awareness is the clearest example: the soul does not know itself by first producing an image and then comparing the image with an unknown original. Its existence is immediately present to itself.

This permits Suhrawardī to take visionary, intuitive, dream, and imaginal perception seriously. Such experience is not automatically infallible, but neither must it be reduced to sensory imitation. The soul can encounter realities through modes of presence appropriate to their order. Suhrawardī even describes a world of "suspended images", whose forms are neither ordinary physical bodies nor merely private ideas. This provides a philosophical basis for an objectively real [[xi-17|imaginal]] or astral domain, as [the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/suhrawardi/) sets out.

Within the Architecture of Hidden Forces, the Light of Lights closely resembles fontal light: the unconditioned source that remains while illumination proceeds. The immaterial lights resemble vertical mediating orders. The world of suspended images corresponds strongly to an objective imaginal field rather than a subjective fantasy chamber. Bodily existence is not unreal, but it is a bounded mode of reception whose opacity both gives form to light and restricts its disclosure.

Suhrawardī also confirms that light can be treated ontologically. It need not remain "merely a metaphor". His light denotes self-manifestation, consciousness, power, presence, and causal superiority. Sensible light is one visible expression of a more universal luminous principle.
