Mesopotamian and ancient Iranian traditions both joined light with divine intelligence, truth, cosmic order, and moral discernment, but they developed these relationships in different ways. In Mesopotamia, solar light becomes the all-seeing vision of Utu-Shamash: the god who crosses the world, witnesses hidden actions, hears the oppressed, and establishes justice. In Iranian religion, light becomes increasingly connected with *aša* — truth, right order, and proper existence — and with the cosmic opposition between the creation of Ahura Mazda and the destructive activity of Angra Mainyu.

These traditions must not be merged into a single "religion of light". Mesopotamian theology remained densely polytheistic, civic, juridical, divinatory, and temple-centred. Iranian religion developed its own distinctive moral, ritual, and eschatological architecture. Yet both recognised a fundamental principle: light does not merely make things visible. It reveals the quality of actions, establishes intelligible order, and makes hidden realities answerable to a higher vision.
