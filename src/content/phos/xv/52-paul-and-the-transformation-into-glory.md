Paul develops illumination as a process of transformation. In 2 Corinthians, he revisits the story of Moses, whose face shone after encountering God. Moses wore a veil, but Paul describes those who turn toward the Lord as beholding divine glory with unveiled faces.

These beholders are "being transformed into the same image from glory to glory". The Greek verb *metamorphoumetha* indicates an ongoing metamorphosis. Contemplation is not passive observation. What the person steadily beholds begins to reorganise the beholder.

The transformation comes through the Spirit. It cannot be manufactured solely through personal imagination or self-assertion. Human participation matters, but the operative power originates beyond the isolated ego.

Paul then deliberately echoes Genesis: the God who commanded light to shine from darkness has shone within human hearts, giving the illumination of the knowledge of divine glory in the face of Christ. Creation and spiritual awakening are parallel acts. The inner person is not simply informed; it is illuminated into a new creation.

The "face of Christ" concentrates divine glory into a relational and embodied revelation. Glory is not an impersonal energy detached from character. It is recognisable through the form of self-giving love, truthfulness, suffering, and resurrection.

## Treasure in earthen vessels

Paul immediately adds that this treasure is carried in earthen vessels. Luminous participation does not eliminate fragility. A person may bear genuine spiritual power while remaining physically vulnerable, psychologically tested, socially persecuted, or ill.

This is essential for the present encyclopaedia. Suffering and [[ix-21|illness]] can sometimes reveal spiritual, psychic, or etheric disorder, but they cannot automatically be treated as proof that divine light is absent. Paul's earthen vessel can be afflicted without being abandoned and struck down without being destroyed.

Transformation into glory therefore occurs through tension. The outer person may decay while the inner person is renewed. The visible architecture may undergo failure even while a deeper luminous form is being strengthened.

Paul also looks toward resurrection as a transformation of embodiment. The perishable is clothed with imperishability, and the body is raised in glory and power. His "spiritual body" is not necessarily a contradiction in terms. It signifies embodiment fully animated and governed by Spirit.

Within the Architecture of Hidden Forces, Pauline transformation describes the gradual establishment of spirit as the governing centre of soul and body. It is neither disembodied escape nor mere psychological optimism. It is a *metamorphōsis* in which the receiver becomes increasingly congruent with the glory it receives.
