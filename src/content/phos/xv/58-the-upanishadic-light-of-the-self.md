The Upanishads turn many earlier Vedic images inward without simply abandoning their ritual and cosmic origins. Fire, sun, breath, sacrifice, space, and light become means of investigating consciousness and the deepest identity of the human being.

In the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad, King Janaka asks Yājñavalkya what serves as the light of a person. At first, the answer is the sun. When the sun has set, fire serves as light. When fire is absent, speech can guide. But when external sources are withdrawn, the Self becomes the person's light.

The progression moves from external illumination toward the principle by which all experience is known. The sun reveals visible objects, but something must also disclose the experience of seeing. The Self is called light because it is not merely another object illuminated within consciousness. It is the [[x-2|witnessing presence]] through which objects, thoughts, memories, dreams, and states of awareness become manifest.

This is especially apparent in dream and deep sleep teachings. When the senses withdraw from external objects, experience does not simply become meaningless. The dreamer generates an interior world of forms, paths, fears, pleasures, and lights. The Upanishadic inquiry therefore asks what remains present through waking, dream, and deep sleep.

## That by which everything shines

Other Upanishads declare that the sun, moon, stars, lightning, and terrestrial fire do not illuminate the supreme reality. Rather, when That shines, everything shines after it; by its light, the whole world is illuminated. This teaching appears in closely related forms in the Kaṭha, Muṇḍaka, and Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣads; [Müller's translation of the Kaṭha](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Sacred_Books_of_the_East/Volume_15/Katha-upanishad) preserves the passage.

The "light" in these passages is not necessarily an infinitely bright object hidden somewhere beyond the stars. It is self-disclosing consciousness: that which does not require a second light to make it known. Sensible lights reveal objects, but awareness reveals sensible lights and the experience of their revelation.

Many later Vedāntic traditions identify this witnessing Self, Ātman, with Brahman, the ultimate reality. Illumination then becomes self-knowledge in its strongest sense. The seeker does not merely observe a divine light situated elsewhere; the ground of the seeker's own awareness is recognised as nonseparate from the ground of reality.

Different Vedāntic schools interpret this relation differently. Advaita emphasises nonduality between Ātman and Brahman. Viśiṣṭādvaita understands the self as real and inseparably dependent upon the supreme Lord rather than simply identical with God. Dvaita preserves a stronger distinction between individual soul and divine source. "The light of the Self" therefore belongs to a field of Indian debate rather than one uncontested doctrine.

## Aphanic light

Within the present system, the Upanishadic Self resembles aphanic light: the light that illuminates without appearing as one more object among the illuminated. It cannot be located by searching the visual field because it is the condition through which a visual field is experienced.

This offers a profound model for the relation between spirit and consciousness. Spirit is not merely the brightest content inside the mind. It is prior to the distinction between perceiver and perceived, while soul is the interior field within which particular experiences, memories, desires, and symbols become meaningful.

The Upanishadic teaching can therefore be expressed as a reversal of attention. Ordinarily, the ray of awareness travels outward toward objects. [[x-22|Self-knowledge]] turns the ray toward its source - not to make the source into another object, but to recognise the luminous ground from which the ray proceeds.
