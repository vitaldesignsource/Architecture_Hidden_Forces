Abū Ḥāmid al-Ghazālī's *Mishkāt al-Anwār*, or The Niche of Lights, is one of the most profound Islamic interpretations of the Qur'anic Light Verse. Al-Ghazālī begins with sensible light but progressively moves inward. Ordinary light makes visible objects perceptible to the eye. Yet the eye possesses limitations: it cannot see itself, cannot see what lies behind an obstruction, can be overwhelmed by excessive brightness, and may misjudge distance, movement, size, and position.

The intellect is consequently a higher light. It perceives intelligible relations that sensible vision cannot see. It recognises universals, causal structures, meanings, contradictions, and truths extending beyond the immediate sensory field. But intellectual light is itself not absolute. Human reasoning can be obscured by appetite, imagination, pride, habit, imitation, and attachment. It also depends upon illumination it did not give itself.

## The ascending series

Al-Ghazālī therefore establishes an ascending series of lights. Sensible light reveals visible forms. [[x-13|Imagination]] preserves and recombines their images. Intellect penetrates toward meaning. Reflective power draws relations and conclusions. Prophetic spirit receives truths exceeding unaided discursive reasoning. Each higher light illuminates the level beneath it while receiving from a light above.

He interprets the architecture of the Qur'anic parable as a map of human faculties. The niche is associated with the sensory spirit and its openings into the world. The glass corresponds to imagination: dense enough to hold images but potentially transparent to higher meaning. The lamp signifies intellectual spirit. The blessed tree represents reflective activity with its branching relations. The oil corresponds to prophetic spirit, so intensely prepared for illumination that it approaches luminosity before an external flame touches it.

These are not isolated compartments. They form a single vertical organ of revelation. Sensation supplies images; imagination preserves and mediates them; intellect discerns their structure; reflection connects them; prophetic illumination reveals their relation to realities beyond ordinary inference. A healthy soul does not abolish its lower faculties but makes them transparent to the higher.

For al-Ghazālī, however, even the greatest created intelligence possesses only borrowed light. God alone is light in the fullest and non-derivative sense. Every other luminous being receives its power of manifestation. Created lights do not stand beside the divine source as independent suns; they are illuminated according to their capacity and transmit what they have received. [The complete English text is available in W. H. T. Gairdner's translation](https://archive.org/details/MishkatalanwaarNicheOfLightsAlghazaliGairdner).

## The veils

Al-Ghazālī also discusses veils. Some are veils of darkness: appetite, egotism, fixation upon the sensible world, or refusal of truth. Others are veils of mixed light and darkness: genuine but incomplete ideas that become obstacles when treated as final. Most subtly, there are veils of light. A real spiritual disclosure may conceal the ultimate source when the receiver becomes attached to the disclosure itself. Angels, intellects, cosmic powers, sacred images, and luminous states may reveal God while simultaneously veiling God if treated as self-sufficient - a form of [[vi-14|obscuration by form]].

This distinction is crucial to the present system. Counterfeit light is not the only danger. Partial light absolutised becomes a veil. A genuine vision can be real yet misinterpreted. A spiritual being may truly appear while the human observer mistakes its particular station for the highest order. The proper question is not simply, "Was there light?" but, "What order of light was present, through what faculty was it received, and toward what did it direct the soul?"
