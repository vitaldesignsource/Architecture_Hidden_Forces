Euclid's *Optics*, composed around the beginning of the Hellenistic period, transforms vision into a geometrical problem. Rather than focusing primarily upon the material composition of light or the physiology of the eye, Euclid asks how visual lines relate observer, object, angle, apparent size, clarity, and position.

The work begins from postulates. Visual rays extend in straight lines from the eye. Together they form a cone whose vertex lies at the eye and whose base encompasses the visible field. Objects upon which the rays fall are seen; objects missed by them are not. Things seen under a larger angle appear larger, while those seen under a smaller angle appear smaller.

## The idealised ray

The extramission language of rays proceeding from the eye should not be interpreted too quickly as a literal claim about glowing beams. In Euclid's mathematical treatment, the visual [[iii-4|ray]] functions as an idealised line establishing direction between a viewing point and an object. The work is concerned chiefly with geometrical relations rather than with explaining what physically travels along the line.

This abstraction was extraordinarily powerful. It made it possible to analyse perspective, apparent size, the visibility of distant objects, the perception of height and depth, and changes caused by movement of the observer. The central structure is the visual cone, a bundle of rectilinear lines extending from a point within the eye, as [A. Mark Smith's "Optics to the Time of Kepler"](https://lps.library.cmu.edu/ETHOS/article/id/629/) sets out.

Euclid understood that apparent size does not vary with distance according to a simple linear proportion. What matters is the visual angle subtended at the eye. Two objects of equal physical size can appear unequal because they occupy different angular relations to the observer.

The work also proposes that very small objects may fall between discrete visual rays and remain unseen until the eye moves. Although this is not modern optics, it represents an attempt to explain visual resolution geometrically.

Euclid's great achievement was to separate ray geometry from the unresolved question of the ray's physical nature. This allowed later thinkers to adopt his geometrical constructions while disagreeing about whether vision proceeds outward from the eye, inward from the object, or through some reciprocal process.

Within the Architecture of Hidden Forces, Euclidean optics supplies the geometry of [[xiii-8|orientation]]. A ray establishes a directed relation; an aperture selects; a cone defines a [[iii-6|field]]; angular magnitude shapes appearance; and changes in the witness alter the world as it is visually presented. It is an early science of how position governs manifestation.
