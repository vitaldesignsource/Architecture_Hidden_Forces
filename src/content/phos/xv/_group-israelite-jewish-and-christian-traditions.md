Across Israelite scripture, Second Temple literature, rabbinic interpretation, early Christianity, and late antique mystical theology, light becomes one of the principal languages through which divine presence is encountered. It appears as the first ordering power of creation, the fire that guides a people through the wilderness, the radiance surrounding the divine throne, the law governing celestial cycles, the glory revealed in Christ, and the transforming illumination communicated through angels, sacraments, contemplation, and spiritual rebirth.

These traditions do not teach one uniform doctrine of light. The light of Genesis is not automatically identical with rabbinic hidden light, Johannine Logos, the light of the Transfiguration, or the hierarchical illumination of Pseudo-Dionysius. Later writers repeatedly return to earlier texts, however, discovering new dimensions within them. A scriptural image may become a cosmology, a mystical practice, a doctrine of divine manifestation, or an account of humanity's lost and restored luminous body.

The enduring principle is that God is never reducible to any visible radiance. Fire, cloud, throne, sun, angel, temple, human body, and sacred garment can manifest divine glory, but no finite appearance exhausts its source. Light reveals while simultaneously concealing the immeasurable reality from which it proceeds.
