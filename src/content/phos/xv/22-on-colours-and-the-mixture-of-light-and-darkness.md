The short treatise *On Colours* or *De Coloribus* was transmitted in the Aristotelian corpus but is generally considered not to have been written by Aristotle himself. It has sometimes been associated with Aristotle's successors Theophrastus or Strato, but its authorship remains uncertain. It should therefore be called Pseudo-Aristotelian rather than used as uncomplicated evidence of Aristotle's own theory.

The treatise develops colour through relationships among light, darkness, medium, surface, moisture, heat, density, and mixture. White and black function as major poles, while intermediate colours arise through differing proportions, reflections, and material conditions.

## How things darken

Darkness is not always treated as a positive pigment. An object can appear black because light is absent, because little light is returned to the eye, because the material is dense, or because light cannot penetrate its depth. Deep water, thick clouds, and rough or shadowed surfaces become dark for different but related reasons.

Red may arise where bright light encounters a dark or smoky medium, as in glowing coals. Purple can appear when weakened light passes through or reflects from a darkening atmosphere, as at sunrise or sunset. These explanations are not equivalent to modern wavelength science, but they arise from close attention to luminous environments.

The treatise recognises three important participants in colour appearance: the light, the medium through which the light is perceived, and the underlying surface from which it is reflected. Its surviving text explicitly presents colours through this threefold relation; the standard English version remains [Loveday and Forster's 1913 Oxford translation](https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/100609675).

## Living colour

It also observes that colours change under sunlight, firelight, and moonlight. Plant colours develop through moisture, heat, age, and exposure to the sun. Fruits change colour as their internal juices and surrounding warmth change. Animal coloration is similarly interpreted through bodily heat, moisture, nourishment, and material composition.

These explanations join optical and biological colour. Colour is not merely something painted upon a static surface. It is the visible outcome of an organism's or material's internal state interacting with environmental light.

Within the system, *On Colours* provides a strong historical model for [[ii-9|tattvic coloration]]. A visible quality emerges through the meeting of ray, atmosphere, material ground, internal composition, and degree of transparency. Light reveals the receiver, but the receiver also colours the light it returns.
