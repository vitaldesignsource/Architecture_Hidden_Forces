In Sāṃkhya philosophy, manifest Nature or Prakṛti consists of three *guṇas*: *sattva*, *rajas*, and *tamas*. These are not merely personality categories. They are fundamental tendencies operating throughout mind and nature.

Sattva is associated with luminosity, lightness, intelligibility, harmony, buoyancy, and the capacity to reveal. Rajas is activity, stimulation, passion, projection, and movement. Tamas is heaviness, resistance, obscuration, inertia, and stabilisation.

The three operate together. A physical body requires tamasic solidity, rajasic activity, and sattvic coordination. Thought requires a stable content, movement among ideas, and the illumination through which relation becomes intelligible. No ordinary manifested process consists of one guṇa alone.

## The clarity of the medium

Sattva is luminous because it allows consciousness to be reflected with comparatively little distortion. A still and transparent lake reflects the moon more faithfully than agitated or muddy water. In this analogy, sattva is not the moon and not the ultimate consciousness. It is the clarity of the reflecting medium.

This distinction is vital in classical Sāṃkhya. Puruṣa, consciousness or spirit, is distinct from Prakṛti and its guṇas. Sattva belongs to Nature, including the subtle structures of mind. It can reveal Puruṣa, but it is not itself the transcendent witness.

[The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/sankhya/) describes sattva as the principle of light, goodness, and intelligence, while rajas supplies energy and change and tamas supplies heaviness and obscuration.

Sattva should not be idealised into a condition without limitations. The Bhagavad Gītā teaches that even sattva can bind through attachment to happiness and knowledge. A person may become attached to purity, tranquillity, spiritual identity, or the pleasure of luminous states.

Luminous experience can therefore become a subtle prison when the personality claims it as proof of superiority. Sattva is an excellent vehicle, but attachment to the vehicle prevents final liberation.

## Three conditions of transmission

Within the Architecture of Hidden Forces, the guṇas describe three fundamental conditions of luminous transmission. Sattva provides transparency, rajas provides movement, and tamas provides containment. Distortion arises not from the existence of any one quality but from imbalance and improper relation.

A purely transparent field without enough containment may fail to embody what it receives. A stable form without movement becomes stagnant. Motion without clarity becomes agitation. The truthful vessel must be clear enough to transmit, active enough to circulate, and stable enough to hold.

Sattva is thus not identical with Light itself. It is the quality through which a field becomes receptive to light.
