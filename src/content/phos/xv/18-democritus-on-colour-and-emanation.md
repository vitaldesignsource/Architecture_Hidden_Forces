Democritus developed a radically material account of perception. According to ancient atomism, reality at its deepest physical level consists of atoms and void. Atoms possess size, shape, position, arrangement, and motion, but qualities such as sweetness, bitterness, heat, cold, and colour do not exist in atoms exactly as they appear to human perception.

Objects continually release exceedingly thin atomic films or images called *eidōla*. These images preserve enough of an object's surface organisation to affect the senses. When they travel through the intervening air and reach the eye, they alter the organ and produce visual perception. [The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/democritus/) describes these eidōla as thin layers of atoms continually shed from macroscopic bodies.

Vision is therefore a form of material contact across distance. The eye does not touch the object directly, but it receives an emanation carrying an ordered impression of the object. The visible [[iii-15|image]] is not immaterial; it is a subtle material transmission.

## Emanation before metaphysics

Democritus' use of emanation should not be confused with later metaphysical emanation from a transcendent One. His images are physical products of atomic bodies. Nevertheless, the model establishes a lasting pattern: [[i-12|a source remains where it is]] while something proceeding from it carries its form toward a receiver.

Colour, in this theory, is relational. Democritus is famously reported as holding that by convention there are colours, tastes, heat, and cold, while in reality there are atoms and void. This does not mean colour is an arbitrary hallucination. It means colour arises from the interaction between atomic configurations and sensory organs rather than existing as a simple property possessed by isolated atoms.

Later sources attribute to Democritus several basic colours — including white, black, red, and a greenish or pale category — from which other colours arise through combinations. White was associated with smooth or reflective arrangements, black with roughness and shadow-producing structures, while other colours were explained through atomic shape, order, density, and motion. Because his writings are lost, the surviving reconstructions depend heavily upon critics and commentators.

Democritus introduces a distinction crucial to the history of light: the structure of the world is not identical to its appearance. [[iv-15|Colour]] is real as an event of perception even if atoms are not intrinsically coloured. The perceived quality belongs to a relation among object, emanation, medium, sense organ, and perceiving soul.

Within the system, this anticipates luminous transduction. A pattern leaves a source, crosses a medium, enters a receiver, and becomes another kind of activity. What begins as atomic motion becomes visual experience. The colour appearing in consciousness is neither contained wholly in the object nor created without an external cause.
