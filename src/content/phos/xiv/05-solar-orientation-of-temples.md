A temple may be oriented so that its physical architecture enters into a measured relationship with celestial light. Doorways, corridors, windows, altars, sanctuaries, and sightlines can be arranged to receive sunrise, sunset, solstitial light, equinoctial light, or another astronomically significant event.

Such orientation joins cosmic and terrestrial order. The building becomes an instrument through which a celestial cycle is made locally present. At a designated time, light crosses a threshold, travels through constructed space, and reaches an image or chamber. The event demonstrates that architecture can receive a cosmic ray and guide it toward a particular form.

## One form of right relation

Not every ancient temple was governed by the same solar principle, and orientation must be interpreted within the relevant landscape and tradition. Mountains, rivers, stars, prevailing winds, sacred roads, tombs, and older sanctuaries may also determine alignment. Within the system, solar orientation is best understood as one form of celestial right relation rather than a universal explanation for all sacred architecture.
