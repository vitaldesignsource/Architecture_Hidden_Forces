## Concluding doctrine: what a rite actually does

Ritual does not persuade a power to appear. It establishes the conditions under which a relation can be entered without harm to either party. Everything in this division — the fire, the lamp, the oil and wick, the orientation of a wall, the hour chosen, the mirror, the crystal, the water, the metal, the pigment, the statue, the glass, the name, the sound, the circle — is a way of making some part of that relation explicit and accountable.

This is why the implements repay material study. A lamp is a working model of luminous mediation because it cannot cheat: fuel, vessel, wick, flame, air, and attention are all required, and the failure of any one is visible. A mirror reverses. A crystal concentrates and holds. Incense makes a current perceptible that was already moving. The physics is not a shell around the meaning; it is where the meaning is legible.

The division ends where it must, in [[xiv-24|consecration as the establishment of right relation]] and [[xiv-25|the ethical and psychological safeguards]]. A rite that produces powerful phenomena and damaged people has not succeeded and then unfortunately harmed someone. It has failed, and the phenomena were part of the failure.

Consecration is therefore never finished. A form set apart for a purpose remains consecrated only while it still serves that purpose, and a tradition's oldest and most beautiful vessel can be the emptiest one in the room.

> A rite is judged by the relation it establishes, not by the intensity of what appears within it.
